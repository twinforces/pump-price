# Yields

Locked 2026-09-27. A plant's own gasoline share is not on the website, and it is not in the filing. The hardware is public. The region's actual barrels are public. The plant's actual barrels are not.

## Three different numbers

| What | Where | Grain | What it is |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| What the stills and the crackers can take, and the production capacity of the products | Energy Information Administration, Refinery Capacity Report, tables by individual refinery, Form EIA-820 | Plant | A nameplate as of January 1. Not last month's output. |
| What came out: gasoline, distillate, propane, heavier fuel, asphalt, and the sweet versus sour share of the crude | Securities filings and the quarterly packet. Marathon publishes a company total and three regions. | Company, and region | Actual barrels a day. Not a plant. |
| What that one plant made last month | Form EIA-810, the monthly refinery report | Plant | Collected, then withheld. The published yield percents are national, and by fuel district. |

A refinery website says what the plant is allowed to sell. Marathon's Los Angeles page says it runs heavy California and Alaska crude and makes the state's gasoline and diesel. That is a sentence. It is not a yield.

## What the lever may use

Turning a plant off removes that plant's crude capacity from the capacity report. That number is real and it is per plant.

The gasoline and distillate that disappear with it are the region's yield from the filing, applied to every plant in that region. Marathon's regions are not the same: one runs mostly sour and makes asphalt, one runs mostly sweet, one is the West Coast stack. Using the national 44 percent gasoline for all of them would erase that.

A plant does not get its own yield until a filing or a table prints one. Guessing it from a website adjective is not allowed.

## What we still do not have

A check that every public refiner prints the same regional table Marathon does. One company is enough to know the grain. It is not a complete book.

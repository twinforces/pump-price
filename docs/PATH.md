# From the headline to the pump

Corrected 2026-09-27 against the five-step chain. The shape of the chain is right. Step 2 and step 4 are not.

## The five steps

| Step | What is true | What is not |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. The news price | The number on the news is almost always the front-month futures settlement. West Texas Intermediate on the Chicago exchange can be delivered at Cushing, Oklahoma, if someone holds it to the end. Almost nobody does. Brent on the ICE exchange is settled in cash against an index of physical cargo trades. A physical cargo exists. It is a different print (Dated Brent, a Gulf Coast assessment, a posted price). | "Futures" does not mean the barrel is fictional. It means the headline is a contract for a named grade, month, and place, and most of those contracts never become a delivery. |
| 2. The refinery buys crude | The usual deal is a benchmark plus or minus a differential. Saudi Arabia's official selling price is that differential, reset every month, and it is not one number: Asia is priced off Oman and Dubai, Europe off a Brent average, the United States off the Argus Sour Crude Index. A refiner who wants certainty usually locks the margin (sells gasoline and diesel forward, buys crude forward), not a single frozen crude invoice for the whole run. United States posted prices at the refinery gate change daily. | The price is not fixed for the life of the barrel. Old oil in the tank is still worth today's product price, because the refiner can sell the product into a market that has already moved. Selling it at last month's price is giving it away. |
| 3. The refinery runs it | Crude arrives by pipeline, ship, or rail. The kit (the still, the cracker, the coker) decides which crudes it can eat and how much gasoline versus diesel it can make. The operator still chooses the slate and leans the yields with the season. Product is sold into a bulk market: Gulf Coast or New York Harbor barrels. | The refinery does not hand a tanker truck to the gas station as the normal path, and it does not passively "get" one mix. |
| 4. Getting to the station | Most gasoline moves by pipeline, in batches, to a storage terminal. Some mixing happens in the pipe. At the rack, ethanol (often 10 percent) and additives are blended as the truck loads, because ethanol cannot ride the pipeline. A tank truck then hauls the finished fuel to the station. Stations in one town often load at the same terminal. The station pays that day's rack price, or a branded supply deal tied to it. The Gulf-to-New-York-Harbor pipeline (Colonial) is about two weeks of transit and a large share of East Coast fuel. Florida is mostly waterborne, because that pipeline skips it. | "Someone bought a cheap tanker from the refinery and is still selling that load." The next load is priced at the current rack. |
| 5. The sign, then GasBuddy | The station sets the price on the sign. Fuel margin is thin. The store is where the station makes money. GasBuddy records what stations are charging, from reports and card transactions. It is not the federal weekly survey. | GasBuddy is not the crude market, and it is not every gallon sold. |

## What the "it's still the old cheap oil" story skips

Step 2 does not freeze the crude price. Step 4 does not freeze the station's cost. The harbor gasoline and diesel futures reprice the same day the crude future does. The physical molecule can still be on the water. The price does not wait for it.

Taxes and the California and Oregon fuel spec are real adders. They are already levers. They are not a hidden conspiracy term.

## How long it takes

| Hop | Lag | Grade of this number |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Headline crude future to harbor gasoline and diesel futures | Same day. Often the same hour. | The contracts trade together. Not a fitted lag. |
| A cargo from the Middle East | Weeks. The pricing window is days around loading or delivery, not the day the ship arrives. | Order of magnitude. Not a single voyage table. |
| Through the refinery | Days. | Order of magnitude. |
| Colonial Pipeline, Texas to the New York Harbor area | About two weeks. | Argus, 2026, describing that line. |
| Crude move to the United States retail average | A barrel is 42 gallons, so 1 dollar a barrel is 2.4 cents a gallon if the whole change arrives. The Energy Information Administration's published rule of thumb: about half of that change is at the pump within two weeks, about 80 percent within four. | Arithmetic is exact. The two-week and four-week shares are from an EIA analysis published in 2012. Our fit reestimates them. We do not hardcode 2012. |
| Up versus down | Increases tend to show up faster than decreases. Researchers call this rockets and feathers. One 2017 paper found it in every fuel district except the Midwest. | A documented pattern, not a law, and not our coefficient. |

The 1983 to 2011 state gasoline survey is still a holdout test of this lag. It is not a live input.

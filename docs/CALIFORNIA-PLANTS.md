# California's plants

Locked 2026-09-27. The claim is half right. Crude plants did stop. The state did not run out of refineries.

## What stopped

| Plant | Crude capacity | When | What happened |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Marathon, Martinez | about 161,000 barrels a day | 2020 | Converted. Fat in, renewable diesel out. No gasoline. |
| Phillips 66, Rodeo, with the Santa Maria feeder | about 120,000 | through early 2024 | Same. Crude units retired. Renewable fuels. |
| Phillips 66, Los Angeles (Wilmington and Carson) | 138,700 | October 2025 | Stopped. Not a fat conversion. |
| Valero, Benicia | 145,000 | early 2026 | Stopped. The federal monthly count dropped it in March 2026. |

There are two plants in Martinez. Marathon's converted. PBF's is still a crude refinery, about 156,000 barrels a day.

## What is still boiling oil

The Energy Information Administration counted 12 operable California refineries and 1,499,171 barrels per calendar day on January 1, 2026. Benicia was still in that number. A year earlier the state had 14 plants. At the start of 2021 it had 1,748,171 barrels a day.

The California Energy Commission list dated July 8, 2026, after Benicia stopped, totals 1,338,171 barrels a day of crude capacity. The gasoline plants on that list are Marathon Los Angeles (365,000), Chevron El Segundo (269,000), Chevron Richmond (245,271), PBF Torrance (160,000), PBF Martinez (156,400), Valero Wilmington (85,000), and Kern in Bakersfield (26,000). A few small asphalt plants are in the total and do not make the state's gasoline.

From 1.75 million barrels a day at the start of 2021 to 1.34 million in July 2026 is about a quarter, not the whole set. A line that says "30 percent over five years" is a news summary. The model uses the federal capacity table and the state list, not the summary.

## Why "closed" is the wrong word for half of them

Martinez and Rodeo are the renewable-diesel conversion. The site is occupied. The crude still is not running. Gasoline from those sites is gone. Diesel-from-fat is not diesel-from-crude, and it does not travel as a free substitute for the state's special gasoline.

Los Angeles and Benicia are the plain version. Crude in, fuel out, stopped.

Nobody in the federal write-up says the state flipped a switch and padlocked every gate. The companies stopped operating those crude units. The credits that pay for fat are why the first pair converted. The later pair is a closure. We do not add a motive we have not measured.

## What it does to the price

The West Coast is short of pipes to the Gulf. A lost California plant is not replaced by a Texas plant the way an East Coast plant is. The state's gasoline is a recipe, CARBOB, and the state's diesel is its own grade. A generic cargo does not automatically fit.

The Los Angeles closure was about 5 percent of West Coast crude capacity by itself. Benicia was another 145,000 barrels a day. Fewer plants also means one outage moves the price more. That is a California lever, and a West Coast lever.

It is not the national diesel price. The $6.29 diesel print in September 2026 is the distillate shortage in [WHY.md](WHY.md): the strait, lost supply from Russia, China, and the Middle East, and barrels leaving the country. California makes that local price worse. It does not explain the national one by itself.

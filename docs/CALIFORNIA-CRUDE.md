# California crude

Locked 2026-09-27. The wells and the route are different pictures. Both belong on the California inspector. Neither is a pipe from Texas.

## The wells

The popular line is that the state wants Chevron's wells shut and Chevron wants them open. The filings are narrower than that.

Senate Bill 1137 took effect June 27, 2024, after a referendum was withdrawn. It draws a 3,200-foot health protection zone around homes, schools, and the other sensitive places in the statute. New wells inside that zone are banned, with narrow exceptions. Wells already there stay, under tighter leak and reporting rules. Rework that deepens or redrills a well inside the zone is also restricted. It is not an order to plug every producing well in Kern County.

Idle wells are the shut-in program. An idle well has not produced for a long stretch. Operators must plug a share of them every year. A company with more than 3,000 idle wells has to eliminate at least 15 percent of them a year in 2025, 2026, and 2027. A well idle 20 years or more carries a fee of $22,500, and the fee schedule steps up again in 2029. Wells inside a health protection zone go to the front of the plugging list. Selling a well to a buyer who cannot pay to plug it is blocked by a 2023 statute.

Chevron's own January 2, 2024 notice to the Securities and Exchange Commission says it impaired a portion of its United States upstream assets, primarily in California, because regulatory limits led it to plan less future drilling. The same notice says it expects to keep operating those assets for many years. The 2023 annual report puts the higher impairment at $1.8 billion, mainly from California assets. In December 2025 the state posted an exemption for 16 Chevron sidetracks in the McKittrick and Cymric fields, work on existing wells, not new town-edge drilling.

So the state is closing the door on new wells near people, and on wells that sit idle. Chevron marked the fields down and is still producing the ones that flow. The map should show those as two layers, not one fight.

## How the oil gets there

California refineries are not on the national crude pipe grid. There is no line that matters from Texas or the Gulf.

The Energy Commission counts what the refineries took. In 2024, of 510.6 million barrels:

| From | Barrels | Share |
| --- | --- | --- |
| California | 118.7 million | 23.3 percent |
| Alaska | 67.9 million | 13.3 percent |
| Foreign | 324.0 million | 63.5 percent |

Three routes.

The in-state barrel is mostly heavy oil from the San Joaquin Valley, Kern County, the fields in the section above. It moves on pipes inside California. South on a heated line to the Los Angeles plants. North to the Bay Area plants. It does not cross a state line.

Alaska is a tanker. The Trans-Alaska pipeline ends at Valdez. From there a ship. Because both ends are United States ports, the Jones Act requires a United States-built, United States-flagged, United States-crewed ship. That fleet is small. Alaska crude is medium and sour, which the California plants were built to run.

Foreign oil is also a tanker, and the Jones Act does not apply, because the trip does not start at a United States port. Iraq, Saudi Arabia, Ecuador, and Brazil have been the heavy and medium sour barrels these cokers were built for. A light-sweet barrel from Texas would be the wrong diet even if a pipe existed.

Gasoline is a second island. No product pipe from the Gulf. The state's recipe is not what a Gulf plant makes for everyone else. When a California plant closes, the replacement gallon comes in by ship. A reported workaround is to land a Gulf cargo in a foreign port and then bring it west, so the long leg is not a United States-to-United States voyage. That practice is trade reporting, not a series we have in the file. The missing pipe is the fact.

## What the screen shows

On the California inspector, three arrows into the plants: Kern pipe, Alaska tanker, foreign tanker. A fourth arrow from Texas is drawn as absent, not as a thin line.

Under the arrows, the well layers: producing Kern wells, idle wells on the plug clock, and the 3,200-foot zone where a new well is not allowed. Chevron's write-down is a receipt on that panel, with the sentence that they said they would keep operating.

The national map does not grow a crude pipeline into California to make the picture tidy.
